What it does

Built for a community pharmacy counter, not a hospital IT department.

Medicine database

Nearly 500 medicines across 96 therapeutic classes and 20 dosage forms - tablets, capsules, creams, gels, lotions, ointments, injections, syrups, suspensions, drops, inhalers, respules, sachets and powders. The curated dispensing set carries molecular formula, manufacturer and drug licence number, Indian Pharmacopoeia and BP/USP monograph references, mechanism of action, pharmacokinetics, pregnancy category, and HSN/GST and schedule classification. A further 324 products come straight from the government Jan Aushadhi (PMBJP) catalogue - the same drug from different manufacturers can sit side by side, each record shows where its data came from, and anything imported without full clinical detail is marked as such rather than passed off as verified.

Patients and allergies

Allergy tracking that understands within-class cross-reactivity - a recorded penicillin allergy correctly blocks amoxicillin - plus weight, serum creatinine, hepatic and renal status, and pregnancy or lactation.

Private by design

Patient phone numbers, addresses, allergy and disease histories, and every prescription’s diagnosis and notes are encrypted at rest with a key that lives only on your machine. The database file on disk is not readable patient data - open it outside the app and you see ciphertext. Nothing is uploaded anywhere, ever.

Weight-based dosing

Four published formulas - mg/kg, body surface area, Clark’s rule and Young’s rule - chosen by what is actually known about the patient, with the working shown and both methods compared side by side. Every result is capped at the maximum single dose and the maximum daily total, so a heavy child cannot be handed an adult overdose. See the formulas.

Substitutions

When the first choice is out of stock: same-molecule swaps first, then genuine therapeutic alternatives ranked by how little clinical substitution is needed. Anything unsafe for the patient is excluded, not merely warned about.

Administration cycles

Course length, dose interval, clock times, tapering requirements and missed-dose advice, derived per drug class - so a 5-day antibiotic and a 5-day steroid are not printed as if they were the same thing.

Printed reports

Prescriptions laid out in the classical pharmacopoeial order - superscription, inscription, subscription, signatura - with the statutory Schedule H1 red-box warning, prescriber registration number and dual signature blocks. Your pharmacy name and logo on every sheet.

Inventory

Stock levels, batch and expiry tracking, low-stock alerts, and valuation in rupees. Out-of-stock lines feed the substitution engine, so an empty shelf produces an answer rather than a shrug.

Backups

Automatic snapshots on every launch, plus one-click export and restore. Your records live outside the program folder, so upgrading, reinstalling or moving the app never touches patient data.

It refuses to guess

Most software tells you what you asked for. This tells you what it can prove - and stays quiet about the rest.

Every recommendation passes one gate

The safety engine is deliberately pessimistic. It starts from not proven safe and only relaxes when a check actively passes. A missed contraindication harms a patient; a missed sale costs money. The asymmetry is intentional.

Five verdicts, not a yes/no

  • Contraindicated Do not dispense. There is no override path.
  • Not safe A serious risk for this patient.
  • Caution Pharmacist review needed. Never auto-recommended.
  • Safe Every check passed. The only auto-recommended verdict.
  • Cannot verify A required check could not run. Treated as unsafe.

What it checks

Allergy and cross-reactivity · comorbidity conflicts · serious drug interactions · Beers Criteria for the over-65s · paediatric age limits · pregnancy and lactation · renal dosing by eGFR · hepatic impairment · dose ceilings · form and unit consistency.

How a dose is worked out

Four published formulas, chosen by what is actually known about the patient - and the working is shown, not hidden.

Preferred · weight-based

mg/kg

dose = mg_per_kg × weight_kg

The actual basis of paediatric prescribing. Used whenever the medicine has a published per-kilogram figure and a weight is on record - a 24 kg and a 34 kg eight-year-old are not the same patient, and an age table cannot tell them apart.

15 mg/kg × 27.5 kg = 412.5 mg

Body size

Body surface area

BSA = √(height_cm × weight_kg / 3600)

The Mosteller formula, for medicines dosed per square metre - cytotoxics, some biologicals and paediatric infusions.

height 132 cm, weight 27.5 kg = 0.949 m²

Weight fallback

Clark’s rule

dose = adult_dose × (weight_lb / 150)

Weight-based, for a medicine with no published mg/kg figure. Preferred over an age formula whenever a weight exists.

500 mg × (60.6 lb / 150) = 202.1 mg

Age fallback

Young’s rule

dose = adult_dose × age / (age + 12)

The last resort, used only when no weight is on file. It assumes an average-sized child, so it is labelled as the weaker estimate it is - and the app tells you to record the weight instead.

500 mg × 8 / 20 = 200 mg

Every result shows its working

The method, the formula with real numbers substituted, each step of the arithmetic, the running daily total against the maximum daily dose, and whether a ceiling was applied. You can check the sum by hand rather than taking it on trust.

A missing weight is never guessed

The software does not assume a 70 kg adult. If a weight is not on record it says so, uses an age estimate, and labels the result as an estimate - and states that recording the weight would give an accurate mg/kg dose instead.

Two ceilings, always applied

mg/kg extrapolates badly in a heavy child: 15 mg/kg for a 45 kg twelve-year-old is 675 mg, more than an adult takes. So each dose is capped at the maximum single dose and the maximum daily total, and both caps are reported.

Why the download is so small

The setup file does not contain the application. It knows where to get it, downloads it from the release over HTTPS, and checks the download against a SHA-256 hash compiled into the setup file itself before writing anything to disk. A truncated or tampered download fails that check and is refused rather than installed.

That is why the setup file is a few hundred kilobytes instead of fifteen megabytes - and why the hash mismatch message, if you ever see it, means the download was corrupted in transit and nothing was changed on your computer.

Windows SmartScreen

The installer is not code-signed, so Windows may show a SmartScreen warning on first run. Click More info, then Run anyway. Code signing requires a paid certificate.

Where your data lives

Your records are stored at %LOCALAPPDATA%\PharmMS\pharmacy.db - deliberately outside the program folder. Upgrading, reinstalling or moving the application never touches patient data, and uninstalling asks before deleting anything.

Source code access

The source is available to review - on request, and not as a free download.

Access by request

PharmMS is released as a finished application. The source sits in a private repository and is shared with people who have a specific reason to see it - a pharmacy integrating it, a developer doing due diligence, or a reviewer checking the safety logic.

Why not simply public?

A pharmacy system carries clinical safety rules, licence-key handling and a curated drug database. Publishing all of that openly invites silent forks that look authoritative but have had their safety rules changed - which is the one failure mode that could actually reach a patient. Keeping a single maintained copy is a safety decision, not a secrecy one.

What you get on request

  • Read access to the full repository - Flask API, React interface, dosing engine, safety engine, test suites and the data set.
  • The audit trail - commit history and the signed integrity seals that let you verify a build has not been altered.
  • A written permission stating what you may do with it.

Questions

Does it really work with no internet?

Yes. The application makes no internet requests of its own - no CDN fonts, no analytics, no telemetry, no cloud sync. It runs normally with networking completely disabled.

One optional feature can use the network: a drug reference lookup that pulls label data from the FDA's public database. It is off unless you turn it on, sends nothing but the drug name, caches every result so it keeps working offline afterwards, and is never fed into the recommendation engine.

It is attached to the medicine you are already looking at, in the medicine record, rather than running behind your back. It also shows whatever the local formulary holds for the same ingredient, so a molecule marketed in India but not in the United States - which has no FDA label to fetch - still returns the local monograph rather than a blank screen. If it cannot reach the network it says so plainly, rather than showing an empty result that looks like “no warnings”.

Do I need administrator rights?

No. It installs to your own user folder and registers itself under Apps & features so you can uninstall it normally.

What happens to my records when I upgrade?

Nothing. Your database lives outside the program folder, and the installer brings the schema forward in place - adding new columns without touching existing rows. There is a test that proves this against a populated database, because losing patient records during an upgrade would be unacceptable.

Is this a substitute for a pharmacist or prescriber?

No, and it does not pretend to be. It is a record-keeping and decision-support tool. Every calculated dose is an estimate to be verified by a qualified prescriber. Verify medicine data against the current edition of the relevant pharmacopoeia and the manufacturer's labelling before relying on it.

What it will do is refuse to recommend something it cannot verify, and tell you what to record so it can. Absence of a warning is not evidence of safety - the interaction tables are curated and therefore incomplete by nature.

Can I put my own pharmacy name and logo on it?

Yes. Go to the Branding tab, enter your pharmacy name and licence key, and upload your logo and address, phone, drug licence number and GSTIN. Your details then appear on the app's startup screen and on every printed prescription and patient report.

Is the source code available?

Yes, on request. The repository is private by default so that a single maintained copy of the clinical safety rules and the drug database stays authoritative. Open an access request describing who you are and what you need it for, and you will be given read access and the design notes behind the dosing and safety engines.

What you get documented on the way in: the weight-based dosing formulas with their published sources, the eight-step safety gate, the test suites, and how to add medicines in bulk from a CDSCO or Jan Aushadhi catalogue.
